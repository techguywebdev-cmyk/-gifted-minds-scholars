7 signs your child might need a tutor

Your child may benefit from a tutor if homework regularly ends in tears, grades or teacher feedback show they’re falling behind, they’ve lost confidence or started saying “I’m bad at this,” or they learn differently and need more one-on-one attention than a classroom can give. Getting help early, before gaps grow, makes the biggest difference.

Key takeaways

  • Emotional signs (frustration, avoidance) often show up before grades drop.
  • In elementary school, skills build on each other — small gaps grow fast.
  • Tutoring isn’t only for struggling students; it can also provide enrichment.
  • Talk with your child’s teacher and share what you see at home.

1. Homework has become a daily battle

Some resistance is normal. But if homework regularly ends in tears, takes far longer than it should, or your child can’t start without you sitting beside them, it may mean the work is beyond what they can do independently right now.

2. Grades or teacher feedback are slipping

Report cards, progress reports and teacher comments are early warning signs. Notes like “needs support with reading fluency” or “not yet meeting expectations in math” deserve a closer look — the sooner, the better.

3. They’ve lost confidence

Listen for phrases like “I’m just bad at math” or “I hate reading.” When children believe they can’t succeed, they stop trying. Rebuilding confidence through small wins is one of the most valuable things a tutor can do.

4. Foundational skills haven’t clicked

Elementary learning is cumulative. A child who hasn’t mastered letter sounds will struggle to decode; one who doesn’t understand place value will struggle with multi-digit math. Watch for trouble sounding out words, slow reading, or relying on finger counting for basic facts past the expected age.

5. They avoid reading or writing

Children often avoid what feels hard. If your child never picks up a book by choice, rushes through writing with as few words as possible, or melts down at writing assignments, they may need more support with those skills.

6. They learn differently

Children with dyslexia, ADHD or other learning differences often thrive with individualized, multisensory instruction that’s hard to provide in a full classroom. A tutor can adapt pace and methods to the way your child learns best. If your child has an IEP or 504 plan, share it with the tutor so sessions build on it.

7. They’re ahead and bored

Tutoring isn’t only for catching up. A child who finishes work quickly and seems disengaged may need enrichment — more challenging material that keeps them curious and growing.

What should I do next?

  1. Talk to the teacher. Ask what they see in class and where your child needs the most support.
  2. Notice patterns at home. Which subjects cause the most frustration? When does it happen?
  3. Talk with your child. Ask what feels hard — children often know more than we expect.
  4. Find the right fit. Look for an experienced tutor who works with your child’s age group, communicates with you regularly, and makes your child feel safe and encouraged. Compare in-person and online tutoring to decide which format suits your family.
