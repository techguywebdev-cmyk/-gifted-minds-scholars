10 ways to help your child read at home

The best way to help your child read at home is to read with them every day for 15–20 minutes, talk about what you read, practice letter sounds and sight words in short playful bursts, and let them choose books they love. Small, consistent habits matter more than long sessions.

Gifted Minds ScholarsNovember 20, 20266 min read

Key takeaways

  • Short, daily reading time beats occasional long sessions.
  • Younger readers need sounds and sight words; older readers need fluency and comprehension.
  • Conversation about books builds vocabulary and understanding.
  • If reading stays a struggle for months, extra support can help.

1. Read together every day

Set aside 15–20 minutes a day — at bedtime, after school or over breakfast. Reading aloud to your child, even after they can read on their own, models expression and pacing and exposes them to words they can’t yet read themselves.

2. Let your child choose the books

Children read more when they care about the story. Comics, joke books, animal facts and series books all count. Interest builds stamina, and stamina builds skill.

3. Play with letter sounds (Pre-K–1st grade)

For early readers, phonics is the foundation. Try quick games: “I spy something that starts with /b/,” clapping syllables in names, or making silly rhymes. Keep it light and stop while it’s still fun.

4. Practice sight words in short bursts

Sight words like the, said and was appear constantly but don’t always follow phonics rules. Put a few on sticky notes around the house, or turn them into a matching game. Five minutes a day is plenty.

5. Echo and partner reading for fluency

Fluency is reading smoothly, at a natural pace and with expression. Read a sentence aloud and have your child “echo” it back, or take turns reading pages. Re-reading favorite books is great for fluency too.

6. Ask questions before, during and after reading

Comprehension grows through conversation. Before: “What do you think this book is about?” During: “Why do you think she did that?” After: “What was your favorite part?” For older children, ask them to retell the story in their own words.

7. Talk about new words

When you meet an unfamiliar word, pause and talk about it. Use it again later in the day. A rich vocabulary is one of the strongest predictors of reading comprehension in the upper elementary grades.

8. Connect reading and writing

Encourage your child to write shopping lists, cards, or a sentence about their day. Writing reinforces spelling and phonics, and reading their own words back builds confidence.

9. Make reading visible at home

Keep books within easy reach, visit the library, and let your child see you reading too. Children copy what they see.

10. Praise effort, not just accuracy

“You kept going on that tricky word!” builds persistence. When children feel safe making mistakes, they take on harder books — and that’s how they grow.

When should I get extra help with reading?

Every child learns at their own pace, but consider extra support if your child consistently struggles to sound out words, reads much more slowly than classmates, avoids reading, or has trouble remembering what they read — especially if it continues for several months.
